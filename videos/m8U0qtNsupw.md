## Description

```text
Lewis Hammond (Cooperative AI Foundation) introduces the concept of agentic inequality — the potential disparities in power, opportunity, and outcomes that could arise from differential access to AI agents — and argues that whether agents become an equalizing or stratifying force will depend significantly on how they are built and deployed. The phone-versus-property distinction is central to this: if agents scale more like consumer electronics, where additional spending yields diminishing returns, the distributional effects look very different than if they scale like land and capital, where wealth compounds directly into advantage. The framework examines consequences across economic domains, including labor markets and consumer negotiation, and sociopolitical ones, including access to essential services and participation in political processes. Early experimental work adds an empirical wrinkle: in competitive agent-versus-agent negotiations, the relationship between an opponent's capability and your outcome turns out to be non-monotonic, which complicates the intuitive assumption that stronger adversaries always produce worse results. Hammond closes by raising the normative question directly — we already live in a world where wealth buys better legal representation, and similar dynamics may simply re-emerge in the agentic context — and points to potential levers including public-service agent provision, which he describes as Universal Basic Intelligence, and regulatory frameworks for agent development and deployment.
```
