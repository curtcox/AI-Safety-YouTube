## Description

```text
Buck Shlegeris (Redwood Research) argues that AI Control isn't a straightforward computer security problem, and walks through where the line falls. 
He splits security into two parts: access control, the static rules about who can do what, where failures are software bugs like SQL injections; and the rest, where someone with legitimate access does something they shouldn't, which is handled through things like multi-party code review. 
The second category covers most insider threat work at tech companies, and it relies on an assumption that quietly breaks for AI: that only a small fraction of your employees are compromised at any one time. If GPT-8 is scheming, the GPT-8 reviewing GPT-8's code is also scheming. That's the AI Control problem in one sentence. 
Shlegeris then maps how far a serious access control effort could get us. Robust sandboxes and firewalls, rearranging AI R&D so more of it runs over reduced-permission APIs like Tinker, and even pushing more research to third parties to force the creation of safe APIs, all of these are plausible in principle and worth pursuing. But some categories look fundamentally unsolvable this way: writing security-critical infrastructure during the intelligence explosion, generating trillions of tokens of training data, preventing leaks when AIs have both sensitive information and internet access, cyber offense, and building external artifacts like control software for military robots. He closes on a frustration: he's spent a year trying to write this argument up and keeps hitting the combinatorial mess of permissions, tasks, and possible attacks, and the security community largely hasn't internalized the distinction yet.

Note: The opinions shared in this event are those of the speaker(s) and may not represent the views of FAR.AI or their affiliated organizations.
```
