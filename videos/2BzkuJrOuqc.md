## Description

```text
Quintus Kilbourn (Project Sovereign) opens with the 2024 Raspberry Pi bug bounty, where IOActive broke an antifuse-based key storage mechanism long believed to take days of technician work to extract a few bits, and notes that most of their effort went into reverse engineering, work they had wanted to do for years but held back on for fear of being sued. He uses the story to argue that the ideal feedback loop in security, where constructions rest on minimal assumptions and implementations face the highest possible scrutiny, is broken in silicon. The status quo is closed from the firmware down, with high reverse-engineering costs, legal barriers that push academics off commercial silicon, duplicated effort in the absence of open standards, and threat models that exclude microarchitectural side channels entirely. Those frictions are asymmetric: subscription services already give large companies and governments full access. The deeper cost is the loss of third-party verifiability, which forces reliance on social rather than technical assumptions and concentrates trust in a few vendors. He credits OpenTitan and similar efforts while noting their openness stops at the logical level and covers only the root of trust. His closing argument is that industry incentives will not fix this on their own, and that a community serious about AGI-scale problems should be equally serious about the ambitious infrastructure they rest on, starting with a maximally open TEE built for security first.
```
